What is flexible work? The eight arrangements explained

Remote, hybrid, flexitime, compressed hours, four-day weeks, part-time, job-sharing and freelance work — what each means, who it suits and what to ask for.

By the Flex.Work editorial team · Updated October 2026 · 8 min read

“Flexible work” gets used for everything from leaving early on Fridays to never seeing an office again. That vagueness hurts you in a negotiation: if you ask for “more flexibility,” your manager gets to decide what that means. This guide breaks flexible work into eight distinct arrangements so you can name exactly what you want.

Every arrangement changes one or more of three things: where you work, when you work, and how much you work. Keep those three dials in mind and the jargon gets simpler.

1. Remote work

Remote work means your default workplace is not an employer site. Most remote roles still come with rules — a country or state you must live in, core hours, or occasional in-person meetups. When a job says “remote,” check three things: location eligibility (many are “remote, US only” or “remote within the EU”), time-zone expectations, and whether equipment and internet costs are covered.

Suits: people with a reliable home setup, strong written communication and self-direction. Watch for: isolation, blurred boundaries and being overlooked for promotion if your team is mostly on-site.

2. Hybrid work

Hybrid splits the week between an office and somewhere else. It comes in several shapes: fixed anchor days (everyone in on Tuesday and Thursday), a minimum number of days (any three days a week), or team-led patterns where each team decides. Research from the Flex Index found US companies with structured hybrid policies required an average of 2.87 office days in 2025.

See hybrid schedules compared for the pros and cons of each model.

3. Flexitime

Flexitime keeps your total hours but lets you choose your start and finish times around a block of core hours — say 10:00 to 15:00 — when everyone must be available. It is one of the easiest arrangements to get approved because it changes nothing about where work happens. It is especially valuable for school runs, caring responsibilities and avoiding rush-hour commutes.

4. Compressed hours

Compressed hours squeeze a full-time load into fewer days: four 10-hour days, or a 9/80 schedule (80 hours over nine days, so every other Friday is off). Your pay doesn’t change. The trade-off is long days, which can be tiring and hard to combine with childcare pickups.

5. The four-day week

A true four-day week reduces working time without reducing pay. The most common model is 100-80-100: 100% of pay, for 80% of the time, in exchange for 100% of agreed output. It only works when teams cut low-value meetings and redesign how work gets done. Our four-day week guide covers the evidence and how to propose one.

6. Part-time work

Part-time means fewer contracted hours for proportionally lower pay. It is the oldest form of flexible work and the most common in frontline sectors. Good part-time roles have protected hours, pro-rata benefits and the same access to training and promotion as full-time roles. Ask about all three.

7. Job-sharing

Two people split one full-time role, usually with a short handover overlap. Job-sharing makes senior roles possible on reduced hours, which is why it is common in law, healthcare, government and leadership positions. Read more in part-time and job-sharing.

8. Freelance, contract and fractional work

Here you are not an employee: you sell projects, hours or days to clients. Freelancing gives you the most control and the least security. You handle your own taxes, benefits, pension and gaps between contracts. Fractional work is the senior version — a fractional CFO or marketing lead working two days a week for each of several companies. Start with our freelancing starter guide and rate calculator.

Which one should you ask for?

If your main problem is…Start by asking for
A long or expensive commuteHybrid or remote
Fixed school or care schedulesFlexitime or part-time
Burnout but you can’t take a pay cutFour-day week trial or compressed hours
Wanting more time for a side businessPart-time, compressed hours or freelance
Living far from any employer hubFully remote
