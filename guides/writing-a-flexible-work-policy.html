How to write a flexible work policy (with a free template)

What every flexible work policy should cover — eligibility, arrangements, requests, location, equipment, security and fairness — plus a generator.

By the Flex.Work editorial team · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

A clear policy prevents the two biggest flexible-work problems: inconsistency, where each manager decides differently, and ambiguity, where nobody knows what’s allowed. Here is what it should cover.

The twelve sections

  1. Purpose: why the company offers flexibility.
  2. Scope and eligibility: who it covers, and which roles need a fixed site or schedule and why.
  3. Working model: remote-first, structured hybrid, team-led or office-based with options.
  4. Available arrangements: remote, hybrid, flexitime, compressed hours, part-time, job-sharing, four-day week.
  5. Core hours and availability.
  6. How to request: who to ask, what to include, response deadlines and an appeal route.
  7. Trial periods and reviews.
  8. Location rules: countries, states or provinces where work is allowed, and approval for working abroad.
  9. Equipment and expenses: what is provided, stipends and responsibilities.
  10. Security and confidentiality.
  11. Wellbeing and fairness: equal access to development and promotion.
  12. Changes: how the policy or individual arrangements can change, with notice.

Common mistakes

  • Saying “flexibility at manager discretion” with no criteria.
  • Ignoring tax and employment-law rules for people working from another country.
  • Making office days mandatory without giving them a purpose.
  • Forgetting frontline staff, who can often get schedule flexibility even if they can’t work remotely.
