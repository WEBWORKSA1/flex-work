Freelancing starter guide: from first client to steady income

How to price, find clients, write proposals, handle contracts and manage irregular income when you start freelancing.

By the Flex.Work editorial team · Updated October 2026 · 10 min read

Freelancing is the most flexible arrangement there is and the least protected. Upwork’s 2023 research counted 64 million Americans doing freelance work. Those who last treat it as a business from day one. This guide covers the essentials.

1. Choose a narrow offer

“I’m a writer” competes with everyone. “I write case studies for B2B software companies” competes with very few. A narrow offer makes your marketing easier, lets you charge more and makes referrals more likely, because people remember what you do.

2. Price for the income you need

New freelancers usually undercharge because they compare their hourly rate with their old salary divided by hours. That ignores three things:

  • You pay your own taxes, benefits, pension, equipment and software.
  • You won’t bill every hour — admin, sales and gaps between projects take time. Many freelancers bill 50–70% of their working hours.
  • You get no paid holidays or sick leave.

The freelance rate calculator works back from your target income to a minimum hourly and day rate. Once you have experience, move towards project or value-based pricing.

3. Build proof before you need it

Clients buy evidence. Create three portfolio pieces that match your narrow offer, even if the first ones are self-initiated or for a non-profit. Write a short case study for each: the problem, what you did and the result.

4. Find the first clients

  1. Your network: tell former colleagues and managers what you now offer. Most first clients come from people who already trust you.
  2. Marketplaces: good for early volume, but fees and price competition are high.
  3. Direct outreach: a short, specific email to ten well-chosen companies a week beats a hundred generic ones.
  4. Partnerships: agencies and other freelancers often subcontract overflow.
  5. Content: publishing useful work in your niche brings inbound clients over time.

5. Write proposals that win

Lead with the client’s problem, not your biography. Restate the goal, propose a clear scope with deliverables and dates, give one price (or three tiered options), and include one relevant example. Keep it to a page.

6. Always use a contract

A simple contract should cover scope, deliverables, revision limits, payment terms and schedule, deposit, late-payment terms, ownership of the work and how either side can end the agreement. Take a 30–50% deposit on new clients. For anything significant, have a local professional review your template.

7. Manage irregular income

  • Keep a separate business bank account.
  • Set aside a percentage of every payment for taxes in a separate account. Ask a local accountant what percentage fits your situation.
  • Build a buffer of three to six months of expenses.
  • Pay yourself a fixed “salary” from the business account each month.

8. Protect your time

Freelancing can quietly become a job with ten bosses. Set office hours, response-time expectations and a limit on revisions. Say no to work outside your offer unless it pays well enough to be worth the distraction.
